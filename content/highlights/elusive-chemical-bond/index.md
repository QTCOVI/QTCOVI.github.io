> *The role of references and the elusive nature of the chemical bond*,
> **Nature Communications** 13, 3327 (2022). [doi:10.1038/s41467-022-31036-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-31036-6) · open access

## The problem

The chemical bond is the central concept of chemistry, and yet there is no agreed answer to what it physically *is*.
The textbook paradigm says that two fragments approach, their wavefunctions interfere constructively, the electrons'
kinetic energy drops, and a bond forms. The story is attractive, but it rests on choices that are rarely stated:
which fragment states are the starting point, which orbitals interfere, and which bonding model (shared-electron or
donor–acceptor) we decide to apply. Change the reference and the explanation changes with it.

## What we did

We identified two kinds of bias in current bonding theory:

- **Internal references** are built into the wavefunction description. They affect the very notion of interference,
  often presented as the most basic bonding mechanism.
- **External references** decide which bonding model we pick for a given molecule, for example whether C₂F₄ is
  described with shared-electron or donor–acceptor bonds.

{{< figure src="h2plus.jpg" caption="Bonding in H₂⁺, the simplest chemical bond, revisited without reference states. Martín Pendás & Francisco, Nat. Commun. 2022, CC BY 4.0." >}}

Using exactly solvable models, H₂⁺, LiF, acetylene and LiBe⁺, we showed that real-space analyses (QTAIM, IQA and
electron distribution functions) are as close to reference-free as a bonding analysis can get.

{{< figure src="acetylene.jpg" caption="Forming acetylene from two CH fragments. The probabilities of finding different numbers of electrons in each fragment reveal how bonding emerges as the fragments approach. Martín Pendás & Francisco, Nat. Commun. 2022, CC BY 4.0." >}}

## Why it matters

- **Delocalization is the reference-free counterpart of interference** and, we argue, the ultimate root of chemical
  bonding.
- **Atoms in molecules are statistical mixtures.** An atom (or fragment) inside a molecule is best understood as a
  mixture of components that differ in electron number and spin, a picture that only real-space methods make
  quantitative.
- **A unifying language.** Removing the reference dependence reconciles conflicting descriptions of the same molecule
  and gives a common ground for discussing ionic, covalent, dative and exotic bonds.
