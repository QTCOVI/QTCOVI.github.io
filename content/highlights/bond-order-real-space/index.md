## The problem

Bond order (single, double, triple) is one of the most used ideas in chemistry, but it is not a quantum-mechanical
observable. The usual recipes (Wiberg, Mayer, NBO) depend on the basis set or on how orbitals are localized, and they
can disagree with each other. Is there a definition of bond order that follows from the wavefunction alone?

## What we did

With Paul Popelier's group (University of Manchester) we used the **delocalization index δ(A,B)**: the
exchange–correlation density integrated over two topological atoms, which counts the electron pairs they share.
It needs no orbitals, no reference and no localization choices.

{{< figure src="n8-atoms.jpg" caption="Quantum atoms in the N₈ molecule with the delocalization index of each bond. Outeiral et al., Chem. Sci. 2018, CC BY-NC 3.0." >}}

We computed δ for thousands of bonds across a large set of molecules, at Hartree–Fock and correlated (CCSD) levels.

{{< figure src="plateaus.jpg" caption="Distributions of delocalization indices for C–C, C–O, C–N and C–H bonds. The plateaus are the classical single, double and intermediate bonds: Lewis's picture emerges from the wavefunction. Outeiral et al., Chem. Sci. 2018, CC BY-NC 3.0." >}}

## Why it matters

- **Lewis structures come out of quantum mechanics.** Delocalization indices cluster into plateaus that match single,
  double and triple bonds, and resonance effects appear where chemical intuition expects them (benzaldehyde, phenol,
  phenoxide).
- **It settles disputes.** For N₈, a dilithiated cyclopropane and an iron carbonyl complex, δ gives clear bonding
  pictures where other methods disagree.
- **High impact.** With more than 170 citations, it is one of the most cited recent papers on bond orders, and the
  delocalization index is now a standard tool.
